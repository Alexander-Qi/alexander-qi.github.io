### The Story

Hydrogen is the most abundant element in the universe, yet our control over its solution-phase chemistry has always been strikingly asymmetric. Chemists move protons (H⁺), hydrides (H⁻), and molecular dihydrogen (H₂) around with routine precision, and modern photochemistry has made almost every complex carbon- or heteroatom-centered radical accessible under mild visible light. The simplest radical of all—the unbound, free hydrogen atom (H·)—has largely remained stuck in the era of pulse radiolysis, pyrolysis, and vacuum-ultraviolet photolysis.

This project began with a straightforward physical organic question: what actually happens inside a bench-stable molecule in the picoseconds to nanoseconds after it absorbs a visible photon if it is asked to release a free hydrogen atom? On paper, the problem looks like a classic thermodynamic paradox—a covalent bond strong enough to survive ambient storage far exceeds the energy of a visible photon, while weakening it invites spontaneous decomposition in the dark. Even when electronic structure design brings the net thermodynamics into the visible-light window, a deeper kinetic question remains: does the photoexcited molecule simply stretch and snap the bond from its relaxed lowest excited state, or does photochemistry refuse to follow that textbook script?

Working on this system pushed me deeper into ultrafast spectroscopy and kinetic modeling than any previous project. The real challenge turned out not to be collecting transient absorption spectra, but making sure the mechanistic interpretation could survive hostile scrutiny—building kinetic-constraint global models that distinguish real short-lived intermediates from mathematical artifacts, and letting isotope effects, excitation-wavelength dependence, and excited-state electronic-structure calculations cross-examine each other.

### Academic Summary (Preview)

This ongoing mechanistic investigation examines how a visible-light photon can drive the mild, solution-phase liberation of free hydrogen atoms (H·) from a bench-stable molecular precursor, focusing strictly on the underlying photophysics, excited-state energy partitioning, and bond-cleavage dynamics.

A central puzzle in photochemical bond homolysis is the decoupling of ground-state stability from excited-state reactivity. While thermodynamic stabilization of the co-generated open-shell partner can lower the net bond dissociation energy into the visible-light regime, thermodynamic feasibility does not guarantee direct cleavage from a thermalized lowest excited state. Understanding how photoexcited molecules bypass kinetic traps—and how energy is partitioned among radiative decay, internal conversion, intersystem crossing, and bond scission—requires resolving events from the early picosecond relaxation window through the nanosecond regime.

To dissect these dynamics quantitatively, our workflow integrates multi-wavelength femtosecond and nanosecond transient absorption spectroscopy (UV–vis to NIR), time-resolved luminescence, spin-trapping EPR, kinetic isotope effect analysis, and excited-state quantum chemical calculations (TD-DFT and potential energy surface exploration). On the analytical side, we employ kinetically constrained global analysis and multivariate curve resolution coupled with systematic model-falsification and identifiability tests to separate overlapping transient spectral signatures and map competing singlet and triplet reaction channels.

By establishing how non-equilibrium excited-state dynamics and spin multiplicity govern bond cleavage and cage escape, this study aims to formulate general physical-organic principles for controlling reactive open-shell intermediates with low-energy visible light.
